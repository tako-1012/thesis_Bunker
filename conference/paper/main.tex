\begin{abstract}
Shortest-path planners routinely send ground robots across slopes and rough
patches that a cost-aware planner would avoid at small extra distance. We
study a simple and fully specified terrain-aware planner for tracked unmanned
ground vehicles (UGVs): a traversability cost that is normalised by the
robot's own limits (slope limit and roughness saturation), embedded in A* as a
multiplicative edge cost with an admissible heuristic (\TA), followed by a
shortcutting step that provably never increases the integrated cost (\TAS).
Our main contribution is a careful, reproducible evaluation: \nTest{} test
scenarios over four procedurally generated terrain families and three map
scales, a held-out validation split used only to select the cost weights,
planner-independent safety and energy metrics, and paired non-parametric
statistics. Against terrain-unaware \Astar{} and Theta*, \TAS{} changes the median traversability cost along the path by
\costRelTAS\% relative to \Astar{} and never enters terrain steeper than the
slope limit, whereas \Astar{} and Theta* do so in \exposedA\% and
\exposedTheta\% of the scenarios; the median path-length change is
+\lenRelTAS\% and the median change in estimated energy is \energyRelTAS\%.
Compared with a binary-traversability baseline (hard slope constraint only),
the continuous cost further lowers the slope, roughness and climb along the
path (all $p<0.001$, Holm-corrected). A heading-dependent extension that
separates pitch from roll keeps every path within an assumed roll limit of
\rlMain$^\circ$, which shortest-path planners violate in \rlAExposedMain\% of the
scenarios. We also report a joint optimisation of the cost weights, a cost
ablation, infeasible maps, robustness to elevation-map noise and computation
time. Code and data generators are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Field robots in agriculture, construction and disaster response operate on
terrain where the shortest path is often not the best one: a straight line
over a hill or through a rough patch costs energy, increases the risk of
slipping or tipping, and may exceed the vehicle's climbing capability.
Terrain-aware planning addresses this by attaching a traversability cost to
the map and searching for paths that trade distance against terrain
difficulty~\cite{papadakis2013survey,guastella2021review}.

The idea itself is well established---planetary rovers have used
traversability maps for two decades~\cite{goldberg2002gestalt,ferguson2006fielddstar}---and
adding a terrain term to a graph-search cost is, algorithmically, a small
step. What is often less clear in practice is \emph{how much} such a cost
actually buys on a given robot, under which conditions, and at what price
in distance, energy and computation. Answering this requires evaluations
whose metrics are tied to the robot (e.g., distance driven on slopes beyond
its limit), whose baselines include both terrain-unaware and binary
traversability planners, whose parameters are not tuned on the test data,
and whose statistics respect the paired design of planner benchmarks.

This paper provides such an evaluation for a tracked UGV. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A fully specified terrain-aware planner: a slope/roughness cost
normalised by the robot's limits rather than by per-map maxima, combined
with hard constraints, used as a multiplicative edge cost in A* with an
admissible heuristic (\TA), and a cost-aware shortcutting step (\TAS) that
never increases the integrated cost, with a heading-dependent extension
that bounds roll as well as pitch (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item A reproducible benchmark of \nTest{} test and \nVal{} validation
scenarios over four terrain families and three scales, and a joint
optimisation of the cost weights on the validation split only, with
mission-specific selection rules and a stability analysis
(Secs.~\ref{sec:setup},~\ref{sec:sweep}).
\item An evaluation with planner-independent safety and energy metrics and
paired, multiplicity-corrected statistics, including the distance--terrain
trade-off, an ablation of the cost terms, robustness to elevation noise and
computation time (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}
All numbers in this paper are generated from the released raw results by a
single script.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Grid search.} A*~\cite{hart1968astar} finds minimum-cost paths on
grids given an admissible heuristic; D* Lite~\cite{koenig2002dstarlite}
supports efficient replanning. Any-angle planners such as
Theta*~\cite{nash2007theta,nash2010lazy} and Field D*~\cite{ferguson2006fielddstar}
remove the grid's heading restriction; Field D* additionally interpolates
costs across cells and was deployed on Mars rovers.

\textbf{Traversability.} Surveys~\cite{papadakis2013survey,guastella2021review}
distinguish geometric approaches, which derive slope, roughness and step
height from elevation maps or point clouds, from appearance- and
learning-based ones. Elevation mapping with uncertainty~\cite{fankhauser2018elevation}
and SLAM systems such as RTAB-Map~\cite{labbe2019rtabmap} provide the maps on
which such features are computed. Geometric traversability costs have been
used for legged robots~\cite{wermelinger2016legged}, for planning directly on
point clouds~\cite{krusi2017pointclouds} and for energy-efficient paths on
uneven terrain~\cite{ganganath2015energy}.

\textbf{Positioning.} We do not propose a new search algorithm. We contribute
a carefully specified cost and planner for tracked UGVs and, mainly, an
evaluation protocol whose metrics, baselines, data split and statistics make
the benefit of terrain awareness measurable and reproducible.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Elevation map and terrain features}
The environment is a 2.5D elevation map $z(x,y)$ on a grid of resolution
$\Delta=0.5$\,m, as produced, for example, from an RTAB-Map point
cloud~\cite{labbe2019rtabmap}. For each cell we compute the slope
$\theta=\arctan\|\nabla z\|$ (central differences) and the roughness $\rho$,
the local standard deviation (3$\times$3 window) of the residual
$z - G_\sigma * z$ after removing smooth relief with a Gaussian filter
($\sigma=1.5$ cells). Geometric obstacles (rocks) are inflated by the robot's
footprint radius $r_f$.

\subsection{Robot-grounded cost and constraints}
Let $\thmax$ be the robot's admissible slope and $\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}$ a
roughness at which the roughness penalty saturates. The cost of a cell is
\begin{equation}
c = \alpha\,\min\!\Big(\frac{\theta}{\thmax},1\Big) + \beta\,\min\!\Big(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}},1\Big),\quad \alpha+\beta=1,
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
so $c\in[0,1]$. Because the normalisers are robot properties, the same terrain
receives the same cost on every map; normalising by the per-map maximum, as
is sometimes done, makes costs incomparable across maps and lets a single
outlier rescale the whole map. Cells with $\theta>\thmax$ or inside an
inflated obstacle are \emph{lethal}. We use $\thmax=25^\circ$,
$\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}=0.10$\,m and $r_f=0.5$\,m. The weights
$(\alpha,\beta)$ and the terrain weight $w$ below are selected on validation
data (Sec.~\ref{sec:selection}).

\subsection{Terrain-Aware A* (\TA)}
The grid is 8-connected without corner cutting. The cost of moving between
neighbouring free cells $u,v$ at horizontal distance $d_{uv}$ is
\begin{equation}
k(u,v) = d_{uv}\Big(1 + w\,\frac{c_u + c_v}{2}\Big),
\label{eq:edge}
\end{equation}
with terrain weight $w\ge 0$. The path cost $g$ accumulates $k$; the
heuristic is the Euclidean distance to the goal.
\begin{proposition}
For any $w\ge 0$ the Euclidean heuristic is consistent for \eqref{eq:edge};
hence \TA{} returns a minimum-cost path.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $c\ge 0$, $k(u,v)\ge d_{uv}\ge h(u)-h(v)$ by the triangle inequality.
Consistency implies optimality of A*~\cite{hart1968astar}.
\end{proof}
Note that the terrain term must enter the \emph{accumulated} cost $g$: adding
it only to the priority $f=g+h+w\,c(n)$ changes the expansion order but not
the objective that the returned path optimises. With $w=0$, \TA{} reduces to A*
with hard constraints (\HC), i.e., binary traversability.

\subsection{Cost-aware shortcutting (\TAS)}
Grid paths contain heading artefacts. Standard string-pulling removes them
but can cut straight across costly terrain. \TAS{} shortcuts greedily: from
waypoint $p_i$ it extends a straight segment to the farthest $p_j$ such that
(i) the segment is collision-free with respect to lethal cells (a conservative
visibility test that also checks cells touched at boundaries) and (ii) its
integrated cost $\int(1+w\,c)\,ds$ does not exceed the cost of the original
sub-path $p_i\ldots p_j$ evaluated with the same quadrature.
\begin{proposition}
\TAS{} never increases the integrated path cost and never enters a lethal cell.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each accepted shortcut replaces a sub-path by a segment of no greater cost
that passes the visibility test; the result is a concatenation of such
segments.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Roll-aware extension (\TAS-R)}\label{sec:roll}
The cost \eqref{eq:cost} is isotropic: crossing a slope costs as much as
climbing it, although crossing produces roll, which tracked vehicles often
tolerate less than pitch. On a cell with elevation gradient $\nabla z$, moving
in unit direction $\mathbf u$ gives $\tan\psi=|\nabla z\cdot\mathbf u|$ (pitch)
and $\tan\phi=|\nabla z\times\mathbf u|$ (roll). The heading-dependent variant
replaces the slope term of \eqref{eq:cost} by
$\min(\max(\psi/\thmax,\ \phi/\phi_{\max}),1)$, the larger utilisation of the
two limits, and forbids moves with $\psi>\thmax$ or $\phi>\phi_{\max}$. No
weight is added. Since pitch and roll depend only on the move direction, the
8-connected graph is kept, with one multiplier and one constraint mask per
direction; costs stay $\ge$ the distance, so Proposition~1 still holds.
Shortcutting uses the same directional cost and constraints. We call the
result \TA-R and \TAS-R. We use $\phi_{\max}=\rlMain^\circ$, which we consider
representative of dry, loose sand: with the track half-width of our vehicle
model (0.35\,m) and an estimated centre-of-gravity height of about 0.34\,m,
static tip-over occurs only near $45^\circ$, so lateral sliding and sinkage of
the downhill track limit side slopes first. As this value is an assumption, it
is varied between 10$^\circ$ and 25$^\circ$.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Terrains and scenarios}
We generate four terrain families (Fig.~\ref{fig:examples}): \emph{hills}
(fractal relief, median slope $11^\circ$), \emph{ridge} (gentle relief with a
steep ridge across the start--goal line and one or two passes),
\emph{craters} (gentle relief with 6--11 crater-like depressions) and
\emph{rocky} (gentle relief, rough patches and scattered rocks). Relief is
produced by $1/f^\beta$ spectral synthesis and scaled to a target median slope,
so slope statistics are comparable across the three map sizes (50, 100 and
200\,m, i.e., up to 400$\times$400 cells). A centimetre-scale micro relief is
added everywhere. The start lies in the left and the goal in the right band
of the map. A scenario is kept only if a path exists under the hard
constraints, so every planner faces a solvable instance; \nRedraw{} of the
\nTest{} test scenarios needed a redraw. Each (family, size) cell contains
\nSeeds{} test seeds; a disjoint set of seeds (\nVal{} scenarios) forms the
validation split. All randomness is seeded and independent of the Python
hash seed.

\subsection{Planners}
\Astar{} (shortest path, obstacles only), Theta* (Lazy
Theta*~\cite{nash2010lazy}, any-angle shortest path, obstacles only), \HC{}
(shortest path with hard constraints), \TA{} and \TAS. All planners share the
same grid, neighbourhood, data structures and language (Python 3, single
thread), so their computation times are comparable to each other but not to
optimised C++ implementations.

\subsection{Weight selection}\label{sec:selection}
Since $\alpha+\beta=1$, the pair $(w,\alpha)$ sets the slope weight $w\alpha$ and
the roughness weight $w\beta$ independently. We evaluate \TAS{} on a grid
$w\in\{0,0.5,1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}$, $\alpha\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$
(\woNConfigs{} distinct configurations) on the \woNVal{} validation scenarios and
select weights for three mission profiles, using objectives that do not depend
on the weights themselves: \emph{energy}, the minimum median energy change
relative to \Astar; \emph{safety}, the minimum median 95th-percentile slope
under the path subject to a median length increase of at most 10\%; and
\emph{balanced}, the knee of the energy--slope Pareto front, i.e., the front
point closest to the utopia point after min--max normalisation. The balanced
weights, $w=\wMain$ and $\alpha=\alphaMain$, are used in all other experiments.
The test split is never used for selection.

\subsection{Metrics}
All paths are resampled every 0.1\,m and evaluated on the ground-truth map
with the same code: horizontal length; cumulative climb (sum of positive
elevation changes); maximum and 95th-percentile slope under the path;
\emph{steep length}, the distance driven on cells with $\theta>\thmax$;
mean cost $\bar c$ \eqref{eq:cost}; 95th-percentile roughness; and an energy
estimate $E = m g(\mu_r L + H^+)$ with $m=150$\,kg, rolling-resistance
coefficient $\mu_r=0.15$, length $L$ and climb $H^+$ (no regeneration). The
energy model is deliberately simple and is used only for relative
comparisons. For the roll-aware extension we also measure the roll angle
along the direction of travel and the distance driven with roll above
$\phi_{\max}$.

\subsection{Statistics}
Planners are compared on the same scenarios, so we use paired two-sided
Wilcoxon signed-rank tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, report the median paired
difference and the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation $r$~\cite{kerby2014}
as effect size, and correct all \nTests{} tests with Holm's
method~\cite{holm1979}. Distributions are summarised by median and
interquartile range (IQR) because computation times and several metrics are
strongly skewed.

\subsection{Main comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the \nTest{} test scenarios and
Table~\ref{tab:tests} the paired tests. All planners solve every scenario; since the benchmark contains only maps
with a feasible path, this is expected, and infeasible maps are treated
separately below.
The terrain-unaware planners \Astar{} and Theta* enter terrain steeper than
$\thmax$ in \exposedA\% and \exposedTheta\% of the scenarios, driving on
average \steepMeanA\,m and \steepMeanTheta\,m on such slopes. \HC{} avoids
lethal cells by construction but hugs the limit: its median maximum slope is
\maxSlopeAHC$^\circ$ against $\thmax=25^\circ$. \TAS{} keeps a margin: the
median maximum slope under the path is \maxSlopeTAS$^\circ$ and the median
maximum pitch over 1\,m windows is \maxPitchTAS$^\circ$ (\Astar: \maxPitchA$^\circ$,
\HC: \maxPitchAHC$^\circ$).

Relative to \Astar{} on the same scenario, \TAS{} changes the mean
traversability cost by \costRelTAS\%, the cumulative climb by
\climbRelTAS\% and the estimated energy by \energyRelTAS\% (median), at a
median path-length increase of \lenRelTAS\%. Against \HC, the continuous cost
changes the 95th-percentile slope by \dTASAHCpNineFiveslope$^\circ$ and the
climb by \dTASAHCclimb\,m (median paired differences, $|r|\ge 0.98$). Against
Theta*, which produces the shortest paths, \TAS{} is longer by a median of
\dTASThetalengthxy\,m and still uses slightly less energy (median
\dTASThetaenergykj\,kJ, $r=\rTASThetaenergykj$, \pexprTASThetaenergykj): the
extra distance is more than paid for by less climbing.
The shortcutting step matters: compared with plain \TA{}, \TAS{} changes the
length by \dTASTAlengthxy\,m (median) and uses less energy in every scenario
($r=\rTASTAenergykj$), at a small cost increase of \dTASTAmeancost.

\subsection{Weight optimisation}\label{sec:sweep}
Fig.~\ref{fig:weights} shows the validation grid. The terrain weight $w$
controls a smooth trade-off: small weights minimise the energy estimate,
large weights minimise the slope under the path, and no configuration is best
for both. Mixing both cost terms pays off: the pure-roughness cost ($\alpha=0$)
is Pareto-dominated for every $w>0$, and all three selected configurations
use $\alpha\in\{0.5,0.75\}$. Table~\ref{tab:weights} reports the selected
weights on the test split. The energy profile ($w=\woEnergyW$,
$\alpha=\woEnergyAlpha$) changes the energy by \woEnergyEnergy\% and the length
by \woEnergyLen\% relative to \Astar, but leaves the 95th-percentile slope at
\woEnergySlope$^\circ$; the safety profile ($w=\woSafetyW$,
$\alpha=\woSafetyAlpha$) reaches \woSafetySlope$^\circ$ at +\woSafetyLen\% length;
the balanced profile lies in between (\woBalancedEnergy\% energy,
+\woBalancedLen\% length, \woBalancedSlope$^\circ$). Even with the
energy-oriented weights no test path enters terrain steeper than $\thmax$,
because the hard constraint, not the weight, enforces the limit.

The exact optimum is not stable: the balanced configuration is reselected in
\woBalancedBoot\% of \nBoot{} bootstrap resamples of the validation scenarios and in
\woBalancedLofo{} of 4 leave-one-terrain-family-out runs. The alternatives,
however, are grid neighbours (a factor of two in $w$, $\alpha\in\{0.5,0.75\}$)
with nearly identical performance, so the objectives are flat around the
optimum and the planner does not depend on precise tuning. The weight should
be chosen per mission, coarsely.

\subsection{Cost ablation}\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} varies the slope weight $\alpha$ ($\beta=1-\alpha$).
A roughness-only cost ($\alpha=0$) leaves the 95th-percentile slope at
\abSlopeZero$^\circ$; any slope weight $\alpha\ge 0.25$ lowers it to
\abSlopeZeropTwoFive$^\circ$ or less. Conversely, the 95th-percentile roughness
grows from \abRoughZero\,mm to \abRoughOne\,mm as the roughness term is
removed. The two terms thus act on the quantities they are designed for, and
the selected $\alpha=\alphaMain$ lies on the flat part of the slope curve while
retaining most of the roughness avoidance.

\subsection{Maps without a feasible path}
The benchmark keeps only solvable maps, so a success rate of 100\% only
means that every planner returned \emph{some} path. To test the opposite
case we generated \nInfeasible{} additional maps with a steep wall across the
whole map and no pass (10 seeds per map size), not filtered for feasibility.
\TA, \TAS{} and \HC{} correctly report that no admissible path exists in all
of them (\infPathsTA, \infPathsTAS{} and \infPathsAHC{} paths returned), after at most
\infTimeMaxTAS\,s. \Astar{} and Theta*, in contrast, ``succeed'' on all
\nInfeasible{} maps (\infPathsA{} and \infPathsTheta{} paths), but their paths
cross the wall with a mean maximum slope of \infMaxSlopeA$^\circ$ and drive
on average \infSteepA\,m on slopes beyond the limit. For a terrain-unaware
planner, success is therefore not evidence of a usable path.

\subsection{Robustness to elevation noise}
Real elevation maps are noisy. We add i.i.d.\ Gaussian noise
$\sigma_z\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10\}$\,m to the elevation used for planning and
evaluate the resulting paths on the ground truth (Fig.~\ref{fig:noise}).
Noise inflates the estimated slope, so both planners occasionally misjudge
cells near the limit and enter truly steep terrain. Because \TA{} keeps a
margin from $\thmax$, its exposure stays smaller than that of \HC{}
(mean \noiseSteepTAZeropOne\,m vs.\ \noiseSteepAHCZeropOne\,m at
$\sigma_z=0.10$\,m); both remain at or above \noiseSuccTAZeropOne\% success.
The mean cost of \TA{} paths rises from \noiseCostTAZeropZeroTwo{} to
\noiseCostTAZeropOne{}, i.e., the benefit degrades gracefully. Filtering the
map before computing slopes, or using an uncertainty-aware elevation
map~\cite{fankhauser2018elevation}, is the natural remedy.

\subsection{Roll-aware extension}
Table~\ref{tab:roll} evaluates the roll angle along every path. At
$\phi_{\max}=\rlMain^\circ$, \Astar, Theta* and \HC{} exceed the limit in
\rlAExposedMain\%, \rlThetaExposedMain\% and \rlAHCExposedMain\% of the
scenarios: the hard slope constraint alone does not prevent crossing side
slopes. The isotropic \TAS{} avoids almost all of this
exposure because it avoids slopes altogether (\rlTASExposedMain\% of paths,
\rlTASRollLenMain\,m on average), and \TAS-R removes the rest at negligible
cost: the median paired differences to \TAS{} are \rlTestMaxRollD$^\circ$ in
maximum roll, \rlTestLenD\,m in length and \rlTestEnergyD\,kJ in energy, and
the energy change relative to \Astar{} remains \rlTASRDEMain\%. The roll term
matters when the lateral limit is tighter than the slope limit, e.g., on
slippery ground or with a high payload: at $\phi_{\max}=10^\circ$ the isotropic
planner exceeds the limit in \rlTASExposedTen\% of the scenarios, whereas
\TAS-R stays within it (the evaluation finds at most \rlTASRRollLenMaxTen\,m
above it, maximum \rlTASRMaxRollMaxTen$^\circ$, an artefact of resampling
diagonal steps) and correctly reports infeasibility on \rlTASRFailTen{} map.
At $\phi_{\max}=25^\circ=\thmax$ the slope constraint already bounds roll, and
no planner with the hard constraint exceeds it.

\subsection{Computation time}
Terrain awareness has a price in search effort: with $w>0$ the Euclidean
heuristic underestimates the true cost more, so \TA{} expands more nodes than
\Astar. In our single-threaded Python implementation the median time on
100\,m maps (200$\times$200 cells) is \timeTAOneZeroZero\,s for \TA{} and
\timeTASOneZeroZero\,s for \TAS, against \timeAOneZeroZero\,s for \Astar; on
200\,m maps it is \timeTATwoZeroZero\,s and \timeTASTwoZeroZero\,s; the maximum over all scenarios is \timeMaxTAS\,s. All
planners scale similarly with map size; a compiled implementation or
incremental replanning~\cite{koenig2002dstarlite} would cut these times.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textbf{Synthetic terrain.} The terrains are procedural. They are designed to
cover qualitatively different situations (unavoidable relief, a barrier with
passes, local depressions, rough patches) and are scaled by slope, but they
are not measured terrain. The pipeline accepts any elevation map; applying it
to maps built with RTAB-Map~\cite{labbe2019rtabmap} on our tracked UGV is the
next step.

\textbf{Attitude model.} The roll-aware extension uses the terrain gradient
under a point robot and an assumed roll limit; it ignores the vehicle's
footprint, turning on slopes and dynamic effects. A heading state lattice and
a footprint-based attitude estimate would refine it.

\textbf{Energy and dynamics.} The energy estimate ignores speed, slip,
turning losses and regeneration, and no path is executed by a controller.
The reported energy changes should therefore be read as relative indications,
not predictions.

\textbf{Implementation.} Times are from a pure-Python implementation and are
meant for comparison between planners, not as absolute real-time claims.

\section{Conclusion}
A traversability cost normalised by the robot's own limits, embedded in A*
with an admissible heuristic and followed by cost-aware shortcutting, removes
all excursions onto slopes beyond the robot's limit that shortest-path
planners make in most of our scenarios, lowers the mean
traversability cost by a median of \costRelTAS\% and reduces climb and estimated energy, at a median
distance overhead of a few percent. The terrain weight sets a clear
distance--cost--energy trade-off that should be chosen per mission. We
release the generator, planners, raw results and the analysis script that
produces every number in this paper.
